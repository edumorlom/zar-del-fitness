After more than 14 years working as a personal trainer, guiding hundreds of people through their physical transformations, there is one thing I'm more and more sure of: not everyone who wants to change their body has the same opportunities to do it.

It may sound obvious, but I believe our industry still has a very hard time admitting it.

We live in a time when social media is full of trainers explaining what to eat, how many grams of protein to take, which foods to cut out and how to plan the perfect diet to get a certain physique.

It all looks pretty simple on a screen.

But there's a reality that's often left out of that explanation: eating properly also depends on each person's economic, social and personal circumstances.

That's what I want to talk about.

## Your body doesn't know your bank balance, but your diet does

Throughout my career I've worked with people of different ages, professions, incomes and family situations.

I've had clients who can buy absolutely everything they need to follow a nutrition plan. People with access to good food, supplements, health professionals and enough time to plan every meal.

But I've also met people who have to split their income between rent, bills, transportation, their kids and food.

People for whom buying meat, fish, fruit or certain foods every day is a real financial effort.

So when I hear someone say that eating well is simply a matter of willpower, I can't help asking:

**Willpower to do what? To buy something you may not be able to afford?**

Because having the resources and choosing not to take care of your diet is one thing. Wanting to do it and running into real financial limits is something very different.

As a coach, I believe confusing those two situations shows a huge lack of understanding of people.

## Not everyone who eats badly does it by choice

In the fitness world we're remarkably quick to point out other people's mistakes.

That person eats too many carbs, eats too much rice, has too much bread and flour in their diet, doesn't get enough protein.

But we rarely stop to ask what's going on behind those choices.

Sometimes a person eats what they can afford.

Other times they eat whatever is available after working ten or twelve hours.

And there are also people who, even with the right knowledge, don't have the time, the money or the emotional stability to keep up an organized diet.

This doesn't mean we should accept any eating habit as normal or ignore the importance of good nutrition.

It means we need to learn to look at the problem more fully.

Because it wouldn't be right to say that rice, pasta or inexpensive foods are necessarily bad, either. A healthy diet doesn't have to be built only around expensive products.

The real difference lies in being able to consistently access food that is sufficient, varied and suited to each person's needs.

And, unfortunately, that access isn't distributed fairly.

## Training someone isn't just changing their body

This is one of the most important lessons my profession has taught me.

When someone comes to train with me, I'm not just taking on a body that needs to lose fat, build muscle or get fitter.

I'm taking on a person.

A person with responsibilities, worries, money problems, family commitments and a life that goes on after they leave the gym.

That's why I've always believed a good coach has to learn to listen before demanding.

I can't expect all my clients to have the same food budget, the same time to rest or the same availability to train.

Nor can I judge their commitment only by what I see on a plate.

My professional responsibility is to understand those differences and adapt training to each person's real possibilities, working alongside nutrition professionals when needed.

Because there's no point designing an extraordinary program if the person doesn't have what they need to sustain it.

Professional excellence isn't about demanding the same from everyone. It's about knowing what each person needs and how we can help them move forward from where they actually are.

## The fitness industry also needs to rethink its message

There's something that worries me in particular.

We've turned certain bodies into symbols of discipline, success and even personal superiority.

We see someone with an extraordinary physique and automatically assume they have more willpower, more commitment or better habits than people who didn't get that result.

But we rarely ask what resources that person has to maintain that physique.

- Can they afford a personal trainer?
- Do they have nutrition guidance?
- Do they have time to prepare their meals?
- Can they rest properly?
- Do they have the financial stability to sustain the whole process?

Because behind many physical transformations there are also favorable conditions that not everyone has.

Recognizing that doesn't take anything away from people who work hard.

It means understanding that personal effort matters, but it isn't the only factor that determines our results.

I think it's unfair to turn someone's financial means into a supposed measure of their discipline.

And even more unfair to use their appearance to judge their worth as a person.

## Health shouldn't become a competition of privileges

I want to be very clear about this.

Having less money doesn't mean you're doomed to a poor diet or poor fitness.

There are plenty of ways to improve our habits without spending a fortune. We can work with affordable foods, plan our shopping better, make the most of nutritious options and build training routines that fit our reality.

But we also can't ignore that a lack of resources can make that path considerably harder.

That's why, when a client tells me they can't follow something, before assuming they lack commitment, I'd rather understand what's going on.

Sometimes they need more education.

Sometimes they need an alternative.

Sometimes they need to get better organized.

And other times they simply need someone to understand that they're doing the best they can with what they have.

Not every difficulty is solved by telling someone they need to try harder.

## A reflection from my experience

Over all these years I've learned that transforming a body can be an extraordinary process, but transforming the way we understand people is even more important.

Today I can say that my experience as a coach has taught me to value much more than defined abs, a body fat percentage or a certain amount of muscle.

I value the client who, after an exhausting day, finds the strength to train.

The one who tries to improve their habits even on a tight budget.

The one who starts from zero and makes small progress that others might find insignificant.

And also the one who needs to stop, reorganize their life and start again.

Because progress isn't always reflected right away in the mirror.

Sometimes it shows in a decision, in a habit we manage to change or in being able to sustain what once seemed impossible.

As fitness professionals, we need to stop looking only at what people eat and start understanding the conditions they live in, too.

Not everyone starts from the same place. Not everyone has the same resources. And not everyone can move forward at the same speed.

But everyone deserves guidance, respect and real opportunities to improve their health.

In the end, after so many years devoted to this profession, there's one conclusion I want to share:

A coach's true knowledge doesn't only show when they transform the body of someone who has every resource. It also shows when they know how to support, guide and help someone with much less make progress.

Because teaching people to train is part of our job.

**But learning to understand people is what truly makes us better professionals.**
