You start on a Monday. You buy workout clothes, sign up at the gym and decide that this time you mean it. You plan a few meals, show up for your first sessions and start checking the mirror more often.

A week later, the question pops up: *"So when is it going to show?"*

**Sometimes we want the mirror to confirm on Thursday a decision we made on Monday.**

I understand that anxiety. When you finally decide to do something for yourself, you want to feel it's worth it. You want your clothes to fit differently, to get your confidence back and to see that the effort is paying off. The problem starts when urgency takes over the process and you end up demanding changes you're still learning how to build.

As a coach, I want to talk about this because behind that impatience there's usually a lot of hope. And it would be a shame for you to give up on something that could do you good just because you started with an expectation that's impossible to sustain.

**Your decision can change in a day. Your habits need practice, and your body needs time.**

## A plan for your real life

After so many years dedicated to training, I value more and more what a person can sustain in their real life. That early enthusiasm is valuable, but I also want to know what happens when you have a lot of work, when it rains, when you're tired or when someone invites you out to eat.

That's where the plan has to work with you.

If you've never trained, at first you're also learning to get organized, to learn the exercises and to recognize what you can do. If you've never paid attention to what you eat, you're adding decisions you used to handle differently. All of that deserves room.

And when I talk about nutrition, I want to make something clear: you don't need to have lived "on a diet" to start taking care of yourself. You need to learn to eat in a way that supports your goal and that you can sustain.

## When you train and feel nothing changes

Because there's also the person who's been going to the gym for a while and feels nothing is changing.

Maybe this sounds familiar: you train several times a week, set that time aside, pay your membership and put in real effort. But then you handle meals however you can, improvise all day and assume training will make up for any mess.

Or it's the other way around. You choose your food carefully, prepare your meals and feel you're doing everything right. But when you get to the gym, you repeat exercises without knowing why, change your routine based on what you saw on social media or finish the session without any reference for how you're progressing.

**You can be working very hard and still need better direction.**

That calls for a review, not a verdict.

## "I eat well" needs context

"I eat well" is a phrase that needs context. Eating healthily is a good foundation, but it also matters how your eating relates to what you want, to your activity and to your needs. Choosing foods considered healthy doesn't, on its own, tell us whether your eating is well matched to your goal. That's also where a nutrition professional can help.

Training works much the same way. Going to the gym is an important step; making the most of that time means working with a plan, learning technique and adjusting the difficulty as you progress. Sweat, exhaustion or next-day soreness aren't enough to measure the quality of a session.

I want you to understand what you're doing and why. To recognize your progress and to have [someone to turn to](/en/2021/10/12/importance-of-the-right-personal-trainer/) when something isn't working the way you expected.

I also want us to look at the whole process. Your rest, your schedule, stress and how much room you have to get organized are all part of that conversation. It would be too easy to reduce every difficulty to "you lack discipline." Some people need a more realistic plan, more support or adjustments nobody has explained to them yet.

**Taking responsibility for your habits can go hand in hand with treating yourself with respect.**

## What you repeat over time

That's why, when I say your body responds to what you do every day, I mean what you repeat over time. That includes resting. It includes [enjoying a meal with friends](/en/2020/12/15/the-famous-cheat-meal/) and getting back to your routine afterward. It includes having a rough week and picking things up again without feeling you have to punish yourself.

One different meal doesn't erase your effort. Missing a day doesn't turn the whole process into a failure. What can get in your way is treating every setback as a reason to quit and wait, once again, for next Monday.

**Consistency needs room for the life you actually have.**

## Look beyond the mirror

I also invite you to look beyond the mirror. Learning to perform an exercise better, getting stronger or achieving a regularity you didn't have before are all progress worth recognizing. They don't replace your physical goal, but they help you see that a process is underway.

And being patient also means evaluating. If time goes by and you're not moving toward what you want, it's time to review the plan, your nutrition and the difficulties that came up. Good coaching should help you make those adjustments.

My job as a coach includes guiding you, explaining and supporting you so that effort makes sense. Your part is to keep it going and to talk honestly about what you can sustain and what you're struggling with. That's where we can really work.

I want you to look better and to be able to enjoy it. To stop living every attempt as a desperate race and start building a way of taking care of yourself that has a place in your life.

Next time you ask yourself *"when is it going to show?"*, add another question: *"What am I learning to do consistently?"*

Because hope can get you started. What you repeat, review and sustain is what keeps the change going.

**Your body doesn't do rush jobs: it responds to what you do every day. Give it time and habits that match what you expect from it.**
